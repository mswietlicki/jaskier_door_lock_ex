12 V Linear-Actuator Door Lock

ESP32-C3-Zero running ESPHome, DRV8871 H-bridge, INA219 high-side current monitor, MH-MINI-360 buck, off a 4S LiFePO4 pack. End of travel and obstructions are both read out of the motor current — no extra sensors required.

This document covers variant A (one door, door_lock_a.yaml). Variant B puts four independent doors on one board with an ESP32-S3 — same per-door circuit and rules, replicated four times; its own build document is wiring_b.html.

Read this before you power anything U4

Set the buck output first

The MH-MINI-360 ships with its trimpot in an arbitrary position and will happily pass something close to 12 V straight through. The ESP32-C3-Zero's 5V pin accepts 3.7–6 V — anything above that destroys the board's regulator and probably the chip.

Wire U4 to the pack with nothing on its output, measure OUT+ to OUT− with a meter, turn the trimpot until it reads 5.00 V, then power down and connect U1.

Two things a door lock has to get right

Keep a mechanical override — a key cylinder or a thumb turn — that works with the electronics dead. And make sure the actuator cannot trap anyone inside; the bolt must be releasable from the protected side without power.

One design consequence worth flagging up front: the firmware drives the bridge fully on, never PWM. PWM would chop the current the INA219 sees and make stall detection unreliable, so the actuator only ever runs at full speed. If you later want a soft start, you need an RC filter on the sense path and higher thresholds.

Never bridge the shunt

U3 brings VIN+ and VIN− out twice — as large pads for load current and again on the 6-way header. Each pair is the same copper, with a single 0.1 Ω shunt between them. Wire the big pair only and leave the header pins empty.

Land two wires from the same side of the circuit on both footprints and you short across the shunt. The motor still runs normally, so nothing looks broken — but current reads ≈ 0 A forever, every move ends instantly as "current collapsed", and jam detection never fires. Before wiring, check with a meter: VIN+ to VIN− should read about 0.1 Ω, and big-to-small on the same name about 0 Ω.

Optional position feedback Figure 2

The firmware works without these — state survives a reboot in flash. But flash-restored state is a belief, not a measurement: if someone moves the bolt by hand while the board is off, it will be wrong. Two reed or micro switches turn that belief into a fact.

How a move ends Figure 3

Every move is a race between four outcomes. The firmware samples the INA219 at 20 Hz, ignores the first 400 ms of inrush, then needs three consecutive samples past a threshold before it commits — one noisy reading never triggers anything.

Perfboard layout

The schematic is drawn as three horizontal rails because that is how it should be built: a bare 18 AWG +12 V rail along the top, a bare 18 AWG GND rail along the bottom, logic in between. Beyond that:

  • C1 and C2 go at U2's power terminals, not near the battery. Perfboard wire has real inductance, and a 12 V motor switching on at the far end of 30 cm of it will brown out the bridge without them.
  • Keep the motor loop tight. U3 → U2 → motor terminals should be the shortest run on the board. Twist the two motor leads together for their whole length.
  • Star the grounds. Four separate returns land at BAT1 −: U2's motor return, U4's, U1's, U3's. Do not let U1 or U3 ground through U2's return path — that is the single most common cause of nonsense current readings.
  • Put the I²C pair on the opposite side of U1 from the motor wiring, and keep it under ~15 cm. The 10 k pull-ups on the INA219 module are weak; if the bus scan is flaky, add 4.7 k from SDA and SCL to 3V3.
  • Screw terminals for anything leaving the board — pack, motor. Soldered flying leads on perfboard fail at the joint under vibration, and a door slams a lot.
  • Fuse before everything. F1 is the only thing between a 4S LiFePO₄ pack and a shorted perfboard trace; that pack will deliver tens of amps into a fault without noticing.

Commissioning

In this order. Steps 1–4 happen with the motor disconnected.

  1. Set the buck. U4 on the pack, nothing on its output, meter on OUT+/OUT−, trim to 5.00 V. Power down.
  2. Bring up U1 alone. Flash door_lock_a.yaml over USB-C, then power it from U4 and confirm it joins Wi-Fi and Home Assistant.
  3. Confirm I²C. The log should print Found i2c device at address 0x40 at boot. If it doesn't, add 4.7 k pull-ups before doing anything else.
  4. Check the sense path. With the motor unplugged but the 12 V branch live, the supply voltage sensor should read the pack voltage and motor current should read a few milliamps.
  5. Verify direction. Connect the motor. Press Jog unlock — a fixed 400 ms nudge with no protection logic. If the bolt goes the wrong way, swap the two motor leads at U2.
  6. Measure the real currents. Watch motor current while jogging repeatedly, and note the free-running value. Then hold the bolt and jog again to confirm the ~0.5 A stall.
  7. Set the thresholds from what you measured (next section).
  8. Measure the stroke. One full move; read last move duration. Set min travel time to about 70 % of it and max travel time to about 150 %.
  9. Test the jam path. Block the bolt with something soft mid-travel and command a move. It must stop, reverse out, report Jammed, and set the fault sensor.

Setting the thresholds

All six are Home Assistant number entities, stored in flash — no reflashing to tune. What matters is that the free-running current sits clearly between idle current and stall current:

EntityDefaultHow to pick it
Idle current0.04 AWell below free-running, above the standing draw with the motor stopped. Three samples under this = limit switch opened.
Stall current0.40 ARoughly midway between free-running and the 0.5 A stall. Raise it if normal moves trip a false jam.
Hard current limit0.60 AJust above stall. Trips on a single sample, no debounce — the fast path for a hard mechanical block.
Inrush blanking400 msLong enough to cover the startup spike. Shorten it only if you can see the spike is over sooner in the logs.
Min travel time1500 ms≈ 70 % of the measured full stroke. Below this, a stall means an obstruction.
Max travel time9000 ms≈ 150 % of the measured full stroke. Reaching it is a timeout fault.
If free-running current is close to stall current

A 0.5 A stall is low, which means the gap between running and stalled may be narrow — and that is what the whole detection scheme rests on. If you measure, say, 0.35 A running against 0.50 A stalled, current alone is too coarse: fit SW1/SW2 from Figure 2 and let the switches call end-of-travel, leaving current detection purely as the jam guard.

Alerting on a failed move

Two independent hooks, so pick whichever fits your setup. The lock entity going to jammed:

automation:
  - alias: Door lock jammed
    trigger:
      - platform: state
        entity_id: lock.door_lock
        to: "jammed"
    action:
      - service: notify.mobile_app_phone
        data:
          title: Door lock failed
          message: >-
            {{ states('sensor.door_lock_last_result') }} —
            peak {{ states('sensor.door_lock_last_move_peak_current') }} A

…or the event the firmware fires, which carries the detail as attributes:

automation:
  - alias: Door lock fault event
    trigger:
      - platform: event
        event_type: esphome.door_lock_fault
    action:
      - service: notify.mobile_app_phone
        data:
          title: "Door lock: {{ trigger.event.data.reason }}"
          message: >-
            While {{ trigger.event.data.direction }} —
            {{ trigger.event.data.peak_current }} A after
            {{ trigger.event.data.travel_ms }} ms

Firmware notes

The config lives in door_lock_a.yaml; copy secrets.yaml.example to secrets.yaml and fill it in first. A few decisions in there that are easy to undo by accident:

  • No PWM on the bridge. output: gpio, not ledc. Switching to PWM chops the current the shunt sees and breaks every threshold in Figure 3.
  • Both inputs low means coast, and the DRV8871 drops into low-power sleep after about a millisecond of it — which is what you want between moves on a battery. The firmware never uses the brake state (both inputs high).
  • The fast current sensor is internal: true and polled at 50 ms. Everything Home Assistant sees is a slower derived copy, so the API isn't flooded with 20 values a second.
  • A 500 ms watchdog interval force-coasts the bridge if it is ever still energised well past max travel time, independently of the move script. Combined with R1/R2 holding the inputs down through reset, there is no state in which the actuator runs unattended.
  • Reversing after a jam is time-based — it drives back out for as long as it drove in, capped at 4 s. That path has no stall detection of its own, deliberately: if the obstruction is on the retreat side too, you want the fault raised rather than an infinite shuffle.
